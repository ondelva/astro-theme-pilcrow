Ask most people why serifs exist and you will get one of two answers: that they help you read, or that a Roman stonecutter got tired of his chisel skidding at the end of a stroke. Both are partly true. The serif is one of those design features that accumulated its justification after the fact. It was invented for one reason, kept for another, and is now defended for a third.

## Stone, brush, and the accidental foot

The earliest serifs we know of appear on Roman monumental inscriptions, most famously the base of Trajan's Column, carved around 113 CE. The letters were not chiseled freehand; they were painted onto the stone first with a flat brush, and a flat brush dragged in a straight line naturally flares and thins at the ends of a stroke. The stonecutter then followed the brushed outline exactly. The serif, in other words, began as a _brush_ mark preserved in stone — a fossil of a tool that is not the one people usually blame.

### Why it survived the printing press

When Johannes Gutenberg and the punchcutters who followed him began designing metal type in the fifteenth century, they were not inventing letterforms from scratch. They were translating the humanist manuscript hands of Italian scribes, which had themselves inherited the proportions of those Roman inscriptions. The serif came along for the ride, and once it was in the metal, it stayed there for a very practical reason: at small sizes and on rough paper, a serif gives each letter a horizontal foot to stand on, which helps the eye track along a line of text instead of drifting off it. Whether this is why serif faces are still considered easier to read in long printed passages, or whether we have simply been trained by five centuries of habit, is a debate typographers still have at length, usually after the second drink at a conference dinner.

#### A brief, biased list of turning points

- **1470** — Nicolas Jenson cuts a roman typeface in Venice that later designers spend five hundred years trying to improve on and mostly fail to.
- **1734** — William Caslon's specimen sheet gives English printing a serif face so reliable that "when in doubt, use Caslon" became genuine trade advice.
- **1780s** — Giambattista Bodoni pushes the contrast between thick and thin strokes to an extreme, producing serifs so fine they are almost hairline: elegant on a title page, brutal in a paragraph.
- **1957** — Helvetica arrives, sans serif and confident, and serif type suddenly needs a defense it had never previously required.

## The screen changes the argument

For most of typographic history, the serif-versus-sans debate was a matter of paper, ink, and printing tolerance. Low-resolution screens briefly settled the argument in the sans camp, because a serif's fine hairline simply fell apart at 72 dots per inch. High-density displays have quietly reopened the question, and current practice mostly comes down to voice rather than legibility: sans for interfaces and short UI labels, serif for anything meant to be read the way this sentence is being read, at length, by someone who is not in a hurry.

If you want to see the argument made without a single word of theory, look at any well-set edition of a classic novel next to the app you are probably reading this on. One was built for a page you sink into. The other was built for a screen you glance at. The serif, four hundred years after it stopped being a brush mark, is still doing roughly the job it always did — telling your eye where one letter ends and stops long enough for the next one to begin.
