Every generation of note-takers convinces itself it has invented something new, and every generation is, to a rounding error, wrong. The commonplace book, a single volume into which a reader copied passages, recipes, and arguments worth keeping under headings of their own devising, dates back at least to the Renaissance and was still standard practice among serious readers well into the nineteenth century. John Locke wrote a whole method for indexing one. What we now call a "second brain," a "Zettelkasten," or a "digital garden" is mostly the same idea wearing a new label, occasionally with the actually useful part — a stable, personal index — quietly dropped.

## What the old method got right

The commonplace book was never meant to be a record of everything you read. It was a record of everything you _wanted to keep_, sorted by theme rather than source. A heading like "On Friendship" might collect a line from Cicero, an observation from a sermon, and something a friend said at dinner, all next to one another because they were about the same thing, regardless of where they came from.

1. You read something and noticed it mattered to you, for reasons you may not yet have been able to explain.
2. You copied it out by hand, which is slower than highlighting and, not coincidentally, harder to forget.
3. You filed it under a heading of your own choosing, not the author's chapter title.
4. Months or years later, you went looking for something on the same theme and found your own past reading waiting for you, already sorted.

The friction in step two is the part modern tools are usually built to remove, and I think that is a mistake. Copying a passage by hand forces a small decision at every clause: keep this, cut that, paraphrase this bit because the original is clumsy. A clipped screenshot makes none of these decisions for you, which means it also teaches you nothing.

## A rough comparison

| Method                | Sorted by             | Retrieval later                 | Friction to capture |
| --------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Commonplace book      | Theme, self-chosen    | Slow, but personally indexed    | High                |
| Highlights in an app  | Source, chapter       | Fast search, weak recall        | Very low            |
| Loose sticky notes    | Nothing in particular | Nearly impossible after a month | Low                 |
| Zettelkasten, digital | Linked notes, tags    | Fast, if the linking was honest | Medium              |

None of these is strictly superior; they trade retrieval speed for depth of engagement in different proportions. The commonplace book sits at the slow extreme on purpose. Copying a passage out by hand takes long enough that you end up arguing with it.

## A weekly version, for anyone who wants to try it without committing to Locke's full apparatus

- [ ] Set aside twenty minutes, once a week, with whatever you have read or copied out that week
- [ ] Pick three passages that still feel true a few days after you first noted them
- [ ] Copy each one, by hand, under a heading that describes the _idea_, not the source
- [ ] Write one sentence of your own beneath each, arguing with it or extending it
- [ ] Resist the urge to file anything under "miscellaneous" — if it does not fit a heading yet, that is useful information about the heading, not the passage

What accumulates, after a year of this, is not a filing system so much as a portrait of a mind returning again and again to the same handful of questions. That portrait is the actual point. The passages you copied are, in the end, just the evidence.
