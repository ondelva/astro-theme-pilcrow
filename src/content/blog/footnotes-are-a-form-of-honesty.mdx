Every footnote is a small admission that the main sentence could not carry everything it needed to[^1]. My first editor called them evidence of a paragraph that had not done its job. I agreed with her for about six years. A sentence that tries to carry its own qualifications, its own asides, and its own citations all at once usually collapses under the weight, becoming the kind of academic prose that makes a reader reread the same clause four times looking for the verb. The footnote lets the main text stay clean and confident while the doubts, the tangents, and the fine print wait quietly below, available to anyone who wants them and invisible to anyone who does not.

## Three jobs, one mark

Not all footnotes are doing the same work, and it helps to know which job a given one is performing before you decide whether it belongs in the text at all or should have been cut entirely.

The first is **citation**: the least interesting kind, mechanically necessary, and the one most readers skip on sight[^2]. The second is **qualification**, where a writer hedges a claim that was true enough to state plainly but not quite true enough to state without caveat. The third, and the one I find myself using most, is **digression**, a genuine tangent that the writer could not bear to cut but also could not justify inserting into the main argument without derailing it.

### A digression, demonstrated rather than described

Here is one now[^3]. It has nothing to do with the argument above; it is here because I wanted to say it, and a footnote is the only honest place to put a thing you want to say but cannot defend as necessary.

## Honesty, specifically

I put "honesty" in the title on purpose. A writer who is certain of every claim in an essay either has an unusually narrow argument or is hiding the uncertainty somewhere the reader cannot see it, folded into a "clearly" or a "without question" that the sentence has not actually earned. The footnote gives that uncertainty somewhere to go that is not the main sentence and is not nowhere. It says, in effect: _I believe the sentence above, but here is the string attached, in case you want to pull on it._

This is different from academic hedging for its own sake, which buries every claim under so many qualifications that the essay ends up saying nothing anyone could disagree with. The goal is not to hedge everything. It is to be honest about exactly which claims need hedging and to keep that honesty out of the reader's way until they go looking for it.

> Cite your doubts as carefully as you cite your sources. A reader forgives an admitted uncertainty far more readily than a discovered one.

Used this way, the footnote is not a defensive maneuver. It is closer to a promise: that the confident sentence above it was earned, and that the writer is willing to show their work to anyone who asks.
