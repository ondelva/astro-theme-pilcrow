Somewhere along the way, "newsletter" stopped meaning a letter and started meaning a content product with a publishing cadence. You can watch the shift happen in the language people use to describe their own work: _cadence_, _content calendar_, _growth loop_. All useful words, none of them describing why anyone would want to receive the thing in their inbox in the first place. I write on no fixed schedule at all, and I want to make the case that this is not laziness — it is the only way I have found to keep the letter part of "newsletter" intact.

## What a schedule optimizes for

A publishing cadence exists to solve a business problem: an audience needs to be reminded you exist, an algorithm needs a steady signal to reward, and a growing list needs to be reassured that its subscription was not a mistake. None of these are illegitimate concerns if you are running a business. But none of them have anything to do with whether you have something worth saying _this week specifically_, and a writer who has internalized the schedule will, eventually, ship a piece with nothing in it just to keep the streak alive. Readers can tell. The tell is not subtle — it shows up as padding, as a thesis restated three times to hit a word count, as an essay that circles its subject without ever landing on it because the deadline arrived before the idea did.

A short, plain example makes this concrete. Compare two subject lines a scheduled writer and an unhurried one might send in the same week:

```text
Scheduled: "5 Productivity Lessons From My Morning Routine (Week 47)"
Unhurried:  "I changed my mind about something, and I want to explain why"
```

The first exists because Tuesday arrived. The second exists because something actually happened in the writer's thinking that they wanted to hand to someone else. You can feel the difference in the open rate, sure, but you feel it more in the reading. One letter fills your time; the other declines to spend it on a week with nothing to say.

## What I do instead

I keep a running file of ideas, most of which never become anything. When one of them has enough weight to survive a week of me arguing with myself about whether it is actually interesting or just novel, I write it. Sometimes that produces two essays in a month. Sometimes it produces none for six weeks, and I let the silence sit there rather than filling it with a `filler-post.md` written purely to keep the address current in people's minds.

This is, I recognize, a luxury that depends on not needing the newsletter to pay rent by itself. I do not have a clean answer for writers who do need exactly that, beyond the observation that the _tension_ between a business's need for cadence and a piece of writing's need for an actual idea is real, and pretending it does not exist is worse than naming it plainly. What I can say is that every newsletter I still open immediately, years after subscribing, is written by someone who seems to have made peace with sending it late, or short, or not at all that month, rather than sending it _on time_ with nothing inside.

A letter, in the old sense, arrived when there was something to say. It is worth asking, every time the reminder to publish goes off, whether that is still true this week. Most readers would rather wait for a real one.
